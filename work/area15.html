The Challenge

AREA15 is a 35-acre immersive entertainment district a few minutes off the Las Vegas Strip. Meow Wolf's Omega Mart. A 200-foot drop ride. A decommissioned 747 turned nightclub. Universal Horror Unleashed, and forty-plus attractions besides. In 2022 it drew 3.23 million visits and finished first among the 2,289 US attractions tracked by location-analytics firm Placer.ai, a million clear of the runner-up.

None of that was on the website. The website read like a directory.

Visitor figures: Placer.ai Points of Interest data for 2022, reported by blooloop.

In February 2026 their VP of Creative brought me in to write the verbal layer of a full site rebuild: roughly thirty pages, every category, every venue, plus an About page that had never existed. The catch was that the site had to be aggressively optimized for search, every headline carrying "Las Vegas," every subhead pulling keyword weight, without ever sounding like it.

The Tension Worth Keeping

Every H1 on the site had to contain a location keyword. Every venue description had to land inside 135 characters. Both are the kind of constraint that quietly turns a website into a spreadsheet: "Restaurants and Bars in Las Vegas." "Shopping in Las Vegas." Parameters that take years off a copywriter's life and make the most surreal place in America sound like a strip mall.

But the assignment was the feeling of walking in. The SEO was only the container.

So I wrote to the container. "Restaurants, Bars & Dining at AREA15 Las Vegas" is the H1, because it has to be. Directly beneath it, where nothing was crawling: Nobody told the kitchens to play it safe.

That became the rule for the whole site. The H1 pays the search tax. The line underneath does the actual work.

The Problem Underneath

Two weeks in, the writing wasn't the bottleneck. The inputs were.

Three sources of truth disagreed: an SEO copy deck built against January wireframes, a Figma system that had moved past it, and a brand voice guide still stuck inside an unfinished rebrand. I asked for the voice guide three times. It never came.

So I named the problem to the CMO, the VP of Creative, and the Director of Digital Marketing, and proposed the fix: keywords live in metadata, copy gets written to the design, everything lands in one living document, pages get sequenced by traffic. That's the version we built. And with no voice guide coming, the site became the voice guide.

The Voice Guide That Didn't Exist

I asked for the brand voice guide three times and it never arrived, so I wrote to a set of rules I was making as I went. These are those rules, reconstructed from thirty pages of finished copy. They are the guide the site ended up writing for itself.

01
Let the place be the loud one

AREA15 is a 200-foot drop ride and a decommissioned 747 with a dance floor in it. The copy is not competing with that. It is pointing at it. Flat delivery, absurd content. The moment the writing starts performing, it sounds like it is trying to convince you, which means the place isn't enough on its own.

UsePlain declaratives. Concrete nouns. Let the fact be the joke.
AvoidExclamation points, "unforgettable," "epic," "like nothing you've ever seen"
02
Lead with the thing that makes someone say wait, what?

Every attraction already had a description in the old CMS and most of them opened by naming a category. A category is the least interesting true thing you can say about a bar with a twenty-five-foot LED maple tree in it. Open on the detail that stops the scroll, then explain.

UseThe specific object: the beakers, the freezer aisle, the drinks that arrive on fire
AvoidOpening on the category: "an immersive art experience," "a unique dining destination"
03
The H1 pays the search tax. The line underneath does the work.

Keyword requirements are non-negotiable and they are dull. Fighting them costs you the argument and the ranking. So concede the H1 completely, write it as flat and crawlable as the SEO deck wants, then buy the freedom back one line down where nothing is being indexed.

Use"Restaurants, Bars & Dining at AREA15 Las Vegas" over "Nobody told the kitchens to play it safe."
AvoidFighting SEO in the headline. Letting SEO reach the subhead.

Every Venue, 135 Characters Each

The food and drink page was the hardest writing on the site and the least glamorous. A dozen tenants, wildly different concepts, one grid, and a hard ceiling of 135 characters per description so the cards wouldn't break. Long enough to be a sentence. Short enough that every word has to earn its slot.

The grid the copy had to fit. Same shape, thirteen different personalities.
Oddwood

A bar with a 25-foot LED maple tree, a cocktail menu written as lab reports, and drinks served in beakers. Take note: some arrive on fire.

Liftoff Bar & Ride

An observation ride 100+ feet up, cocktail in hand, Strip in view. The most civilized way to see Vegas from above.

Rocket Fizz

Over-the-top soda flavors, salt water taffy in every variety, and a candy shop that doesn't ask for permission.

Beast Mobile

Elote, quesadillas, and Mexican street food made to be eaten standing up. Beast Mobile, parked and ready.

Nacho Daddy

Born in Las Vegas. Home to gourmet nachos, award-winning margaritas, and the original scorpion shot — if you dare.

Why it works

At 135 characters there is room for one idea and one proof, so every description had to decide what its single strangest true fact was. "Some arrive on fire" costs six words and does more than any adjective would. The ceiling stopped being a limitation about two days in and started being the edit.

Why it works

Omega Mart's old description explained that it was an immersive art installation, which is accurate and tells you nothing. "It looks like a grocery store. It isn't." is four words shorter and does the whole job, because the confusion is the product.

What changed and what didn't

The client tightened the venue lines toward information and pulled the headline further toward strangeness, which is the right trade in both cases. What survived is the structure: a headline that commits to a bit, then descriptions that pay it off with specifics. A voice system works when other people can edit inside it without breaking it.

How It Was Run

  • Reported to The CMO and the VP of Creative directly. No account layer and no creative director above me. I was the creative direction on language for this build.
  • Worked across A ten-person launch team: marketing, digital, SEO, design QA, CMS, and an outside production consultancy. I was the only writer in the room.
  • Owned Voice, page-level content strategy, page priority sequencing, and the resolution of the SEO-versus-design conflict that was blocking the developer handoff.
  • Cadence Weekly content check-ins escalating to daily stand-ups through the launch window, with copy delivered against a moving target and a shifting launch date.
  • Method Wrote to design, not to deck. Figma as the source of truth for length and hierarchy; keywords handled as metadata; one living document for everything else.
